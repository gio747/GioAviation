747-400F Stabilizer Trim and the Green Band

Why the 747 trims the whole stabilizer, what the green band actually represents, how it is calculated, and how the system protects you from a mistrimmed takeoff.

Part 1 · The explanation

01

Why the 747 trims the stabilizer, not the elevator

The 747 has no elevator trim tab. Pitch trim changes the incidence of the whole horizontal stabilizer. The surface is very large, so a few degrees of incidence produce a large nose-up or nose-down pitching moment.

That is why trim must be correct before takeoff. The elevator is for manoeuvring around the trim setting. It is not meant to make up for a wrong trim.

02

How the stabilizer moves

Two hydraulic actuators move the stabilizer, each with its own stabilizer trim control module (STCM). Hydraulic system 3 powers the left STCM and system 2 powers the right. The two outputs add together mechanically. If one actuator fails, the other keeps trimming alone at half rate.

Four ways to command trim

Control wheel trim switches

The normal method. Both switches must be pushed together, in the same direction. Opposite inputs from Captain and F/O do nothing. Column movement against the trim triggers the column cutout.

Alternate (ALTN) switches

On the aisle stand. They command the STCMs directly, are not stopped by the column cutout, and give the widest trim range.

Autopilot

Trim is automatic. Wheel switches with a single autopilot engaged disconnect it. With more than one engaged they are inhibited. ALTN always overrides and never disconnects the autopilot.

Speed trim

Corrects speed stability automatically in some phases of flight. It has no effect on takeoff.

STAB TRIM CUTOUT switches. Two on the aisle stand, one per channel. In AUTO a switch removes hydraulic power by itself if it detects unscheduled trim. In CUTOUT it removes power at all times.

Indicators. Two, one each side of the thrust levers, scale 0 to 15 units. 0 at the top is full nose down; 15 at the bottom is full nose up. More units means more nose up.

03

What the green band is

The green strip beside the scale shows the trim range acceptable for takeoff at the current weight, CG and thrust. The pointer must be inside it before takeoff.

The key point: the band is not fixed. It moves with the loading.

Forward CG

Nose-heavy. More pitch-up authority is needed at rotation, so you need more nose-up trim.

Aft CG

Rotates more easily. You need less nose-up trim.

The FPPM tables show it clearly. At the same weight, moving the CG from 9% to 33% MAC lowers the required trim by several units. A heavier aircraft needs more trim. Thrust matters too: at many weights and CGs the TO2 derate table calls for more units than full thrust.

The three bands

Green band limits on the 0–15 scale

Trim units. Left is nose down, right is nose up.

Mid-band (always included)Extension, aft CGExtension, forward CG

Mid-band

The basic band for a mid-range CG. On most aircraft it is a painted green stripe.

Nose-up band

Selected for a forward CG. Covers the whole mid-band plus an extra section towards nose up.

Nose-down band

Selected for an aft CG. Covers the whole mid-band plus an extra section towards nose down.

So the mid-band is always there, and the system adds an extension up or down according to CG. The band lights come on only on the ground, with a valid CG or with engines running, and go out above V1.

04

How it is calculated

During FMC preflight you enter the takeoff CG (%MAC) on the TAKEOFF REF page. The FMC already holds gross weight and the selected thrust or derate. From these three inputs it:

1 · Trim
Calculates the takeoff trim and shows it next to the CG entry, in 0.1-unit steps.
2 · Band
Feeds the MAWEA (warning electronics), which decides which of the three green bands to light.
3 · You
Set the trim, cross-check it against the loadsheet, and confirm the pointer is inside the lit band.
05

Two layers of protection

The independent check: nose gear pressure switch

The system does not rely only on what was typed into the FMC. A pressure switch on the nose gear strut senses the CG physically. A forward CG puts more weight on the nose gear, the strut compresses and pressure rises. An aft CG lowers it.

The MAWEA compares the switch with the calculated band. If they disagree, the advisory >STAB GREENBAND appears: the loading, gross weight, CG or thrust entry may be wrong. There is no comparison when the mid-band is selected.

Even when they disagree, the band that stays lit is the one calculated from FMC data. The message is only a prompt to recheck the data.

The safety net: CONFIG STAB

If the advisory is missed, or the trim is simply wrong, setting takeoff thrust with the stabilizer outside the green band triggers >CONFIG STAB with a siren. Conditions: on the ground, below V1, fuel control switches in RUN, takeoff thrust on an inboard engine.

06

In practice

Before Start
The Captain sets takeoff trim and checks it is in the green band. The value is cross-checked against the loadsheet MACTOW.
Runway or data change
Re-enter the CG, reset the trim, recheck the green band.
During rotation
Do not trim. Per the FCTM, trimming during rotation costs you the feel of the elevator, which can lead to too high a rotation rate and a tail strike.
Common sense
The FCTM lists a mistrimmed stabilizer, usually from wrong data, as a tail strike risk factor. The best defence is to check that the loadsheet numbers make sense against your experience.
